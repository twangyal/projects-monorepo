# Gaze Navigator

Operate real websites by looking and speaking. It is for people who find a mouse or keyboard hard to use. A context circle follows your gaze (or the pointer). You say or type what to do, and the navigator acts on the controls inside the circle. It asks when the target is unclear and asks you to confirm consequential actions. The main product is a Chrome (Manifest V3) extension. The website in this folder covers setup, practice and developer labs.

**Status:** ACTIVE — portfolio priority. Direction: [docs/PRODUCT_DIRECTION.md](../../docs/PRODUCT_DIRECTION.md)

## What works today

- **Chrome extension** that runs on the page you are viewing. Turn it on from the toolbar popup or with the suggested shortcut <kbd>Alt</kbd>+<kbd>Shift</kbd>+<kbd>N</kbd>. Two optional settings ask for access to all websites: keep it on in the tab when pages change, and start it automatically on every site.
- **Context circle** driven by the pointer or by webcam gaze. Camera mode finds the face and irises with WebGazer's face model and places the gaze from where each iris sits between its eye's corners and lids, with the head's turn and place, through a ridge regression fitted on the calibration. Until there are nine calibration samples, or when no irises are found, WebGazer's own estimate places it. Leaning or moving well away from where you sat while calibrating (the head's place in the picture or its turn more than three times the calibration's own spread, at least 12% of the picture, for a second and a half) makes the navigator say the circle may be off and suggest moving back or refreshing the calibration, once until you return. Camera mode uses a nine-dot calibration that needs no clicks, plus One Euro smoothing. "Refresh calibration" (or the panel's button) keeps the calibration in use and adds five dots, the corners and the centre, then checks accuracy again; stopped or short of four learned dots, it leaves the calibration in use with any dots it did learn. Four more dots then check the calibration: the circle is sized to hold 9 in 10 of the smoothed gaze samples (small, medium or large), and when even the large circle cannot, the navigator says so and suggests recalibrating or using the pointer. While you work, each small control you name and use, inside the circle or just outside it (within three radii, where webcam gaze often lands short of what you look at), becomes one more calibration sample at its centre (at most one every two seconds), which corrects drift and is saved with the calibration. A delayed learning reply cannot save after recalibration, a window-size or tracking-mode change, or hiding the tab. An explicit missing camera estimate, five seconds without valid estimates, a camera picture frozen for three seconds, or a disconnected camera frame clears the old page target, speech capture and pending confirmation. Valid estimates over the panel keep tracking live while holding the last page area; silence expires it. Returning to a hidden tab also waits for gaze estimated after the tab becomes visible without requiring recalibration. Page actions wait for a fresh gaze on the page; looking at the panel does not reuse the old target. Pause, cancel and pointer/camera recovery remain available. Camera page actions require successful training or a valid restored calibration. Cancelling training or learning too few dots keeps them blocked until recalibration or pointer fallback; cancelling only the optional accuracy check keeps completed training. Changing the active viewport’s width or height cancels training or the accuracy check and blocks camera page actions until you recalibrate or use the pointer. The navigator does not automatically start new dots during resizing. Same-size resize events and circle-size adjustments keep the mapping. In the extension, a calibration is reused on later pages as long as the window size stays the same: as it is for up to two hours, and refreshed with five dots after that.
- **Head pointer**: "use head pointer" (or the Head button under Tracking) makes the circle follow where your nose points instead of your eyes. The webcam's face landmarks (the same face model that finds the eyes) show how the head is turned, and the same no-click calibration, pointing your nose at each dot, maps that to the screen, as do the controls you use afterwards. Turning your head is more work than looking but steadier than webcam eye tracking, for people who can turn their head comfortably. The eyes and the head keep separate calibrations; "use camera" returns to the eyes. It has been tested only with simulated landmarks, not a real camera.
- **Control discovery** inside the circle: links, buttons, fields, dropdowns, checkboxes and ARIA widgets get numbered outlines. Discovery also reaches open shadow roots, same-origin iframes and script-only clickables that set a pointer cursor. In the extension, embedded frames from other sites (sign-up forms, widgets) are reached too when it has access to all websites: their controls join the circle and are acted on inside the frame, and pointer moves over them steer the circle. Without that access they are listed as unreachable.
- **Spoken or typed instructions** through the browser's Web Speech API, or through a typed instruction box. Extension speech runs in its own capture frame, with a microphone setup tab and typed recovery when permission or recognition fails. Camera and listening can be stopped independently. Hiding the tab stops listening, clears pending commands and cancels active calibration dots without discarding completed calibration; choose Start listening when you return. Where Chrome can recognize speech on the device, the navigator uses that once its language pack is installed (settings offer the one-time download): no audio then leaves the device, and recognition favours the names of the controls in and around the circle. Context is captured when you start speaking. If recognition ends without words, or you stop listening, that unfinished capture is released so the next instruction uses a fresh gaze. <kbd>Alt</kbd>+<kbd>Shift</kbd>+<kbd>G</kbd> opens command entry while the page keeps its focus.
- **Help by voice**: ask “what can I say?” for short suggestions about the controls in the circle, a waiting question, or pause/tracking recovery. Help keeps a pending target and its original timeout. During continuous dictation, say “help with the navigator” for help without typing it or ending the mode; ordinary “what can I say” remains dictated text. With speech addressing enabled, begin help with “Navigator”. Replies use the existing Speak feedback setting and never include field contents.
- **Spoken feedback and listening**: when Speak feedback is enabled, wait for the reply to finish before giving the next spoken instruction. Commands, confirmations and dictation captured during that reply are ignored, even if their final words arrive after it ends; immediate pause/stop/cancel and typed or panel controls remain available. Disabling feedback cancels its voice. This uses recognition/output events, not acoustic echo cancellation; real speaker/microphone overlap and delayed recognition still need physical evaluation.
- **Optional speech addressing**: enable **Require “Navigator” before spoken instructions**, or say “require navigator prefix”. Each command, confirmation and dictation phrase then needs that exact address, such as “Navigator, open help”; other recognized phrases are ignored. Pause, stop, cancel and their immediate safety synonyms still work alone. Say “Navigator turn off navigator prefix” or use the setting to turn it off. Typed instructions stay available. The setting persists across pages and changing it retires queued speech and pending targets. This filters recognized text, does not identify the speaker and has not been evaluated against real background speech.
- **Misheard speech**: when the recogniser's first reading is not understood, its other readings of the same speech are tried and the first one understood is used, and the reply says which ("Heard "click sign in""). Commonly misheard command words are corrected ("quick" for click, "chews" for choose), and a control whose name sounds like the one said ("signing" for Sign in, "summit" for Submit) is offered, after a command word or said alone (unless it is a short everyday word: "hello" is not taken for Help): both ask "Did you mean …?", outline the control and act only after confirm, while a corrected "school down" or "cancer" just scrolls or cancels. A name heard run together or split ("login" for Log in, "sign up" for Signup) counts as said exactly. Exact names, including those just outside the circle, come first. With the instruction suite's commands misheard this way (`--misheard`), 13 of 27 are understood as heard and all 27 are understood or offered correctly, against 4 before, with no wrong actions.
- **Offers when not understood**: a request not understood as a command, whose words point to one control or dropdown choice in the circle by a word of its name or a word for what it does or is about, offers it for a yes ("I have an account already": Did you mean "Sign in"?; "can I email you" for Contact us, "bigger print please" for Large print, "move the socks to my saved items" for that row's Save for later). Nothing is offered when the words say not to, ask for an action the control does not do ("delete my account"), point two ways, or name only a text field. A loose match that would do more than was asked, or any match when the words only ask to see something ("open the cart" against Add to cart), is offered rather than pressed, and a password is never typed from speech. A spoken request whose offer gets a yes is learned: said the same way again, it acts on the one control of that name and kind in or just outside the circle without asking (one that may have consequences is still confirmed), and says so. Only spoken requests confirmed by voice are learned, since a page could type into the panel but cannot speak for you. Up to 200 are kept, the latest first: in the extension on this device for every website, on the practice website for the tab's session. "What have you learned?" lists them and "forget what you learned" clears them.
- **Phrases you teach**: say "when I say the usual, I mean subscribe to our newsletter" (a comma or "I mean" separates the two; without either, the phrase ends where a command word or a control's whole name begins), and from then on "the usual" does that instruction wherever you say it, understood afresh on each page, and a control that may have consequences is still confirmed. The instruction must be understood where you teach it, and the navigator's own commands ("yes", "go back") keep their meaning. Phrases are taught only by voice, kept with the learned requests, and "forget the usual" (or "forget what the usual means") forgets one.
- **Navigation**: open or click by name or ordinal ("the second one"), or just say a control's whole name, even one that starts like a command: "find out more", "check availability", "enter the competition", "clear filters" and "turn on notifications" press (or switch on) the control of that name rather than searching, typing or ticking, and one of that name just outside the circle or elsewhere is looked for there before anything is typed. With no checkbox of that name, "check" means look ("check my order status" opens it), and "check out" is the checkout. Scroll the page or a scrolling region, go back or forward, search the page's search field, and submit. Search keeps the captured form destination through query entry: a changed form or pause/cancel stops submission, and a query already entered can be undone. If gaze lands just outside a list, scrolling can recover a unique region touched by the circle; competing regions ask you to look further inside. A named region ("scroll the list down", "the sidebar", "the comments") uses a matching pane under the gaze or the nearest visible match. So does an area named by its own label or heading, wherever the gaze is: "scroll the reviews down" or "scroll down the terms and conditions" scrolls the area whose label, the heading just before it or the first one inside it carries the name (the nearer of two that both do), and a name no area on screen carries scrolls nothing. Thin semantic panes between viewport samples are checked through their painted area, including open shadow roots and same-origin frames. An unrelated pane or page does not move if no region matches. "Scroll the page down" explicitly moves the page.
- **Everyday phrasing**: the navigator's own commands are understood as people say them: "hang on", "give me a minute" (pause); "carry on", "I'm back" (resume); "yep", "yeah go ahead", "sure" (confirm); "nope", "no not that" (decline); "abort", "stop everything" (cancel); "down a bit", "keep scrolling", "all the way down", "back to the top"; "scratch that", "take that back", "wipe the field", "swap Tuesday for Wednesday", "start a new line". Choices can be said without "choose" ("make it hardcover", "switch to the audiobook", "I'll take the paperback"), text can name its field ("write running late in the message", "put Ada in the name field", "fill in the email with …"), and list entries can be counted ("open the second article", "delete the first one"). "Hang on", "hold on", "abort" and "never mind" act as soon as they are heard, like "stop". "Not now" is left for the buttons that say it.
- **Tasks said as what you want**: "put it in my basket", "finish my order", "buy it now", "check out", "time to pay", "what do people think of it", "read the reviews", "how much does it cost", "I need to get in touch", "who can I ring about this", "how much will it set me back", "any reviews worth reading", "log into my account", "take me home" and "back to the start page" reach the control that does it (Add to basket or cart, Place order or Checkout, Reviews, Pricing or Prices, Contact, Sign in, Home); "I'm looking for cookbooks" and "have you got anything on maps" search the site; "I'd like the hardcover", "pick the hardback", "large print please", "I'll collect it myself" and "it's about an order problem" choose; "my name is Ada Lovelace" types into the name field ("remember my name is" still saves it); "get rid of the message" clears that field; "send it" (or "fire it off") presses the form's one Send button unless a field is being typed in; "I'd like a copy of this sent to me" ticks the box whose every word it says (Send me a copy), and a box named without "click" is ticked rather than clicked, so a ticked one stays ticked; "add another one" presses the one Add control there; "click the button" presses the only button in the circle, or asks which; "take the second one out" and "tell me more about the third one" count along a list. Seven held-out sets of such instructions, each written by the same developer before the rules for it, are now 97-100% right, and none is acted on wrongly.
- **Choices said in other words**: a dropdown's option can be named the way people say it. Sizes by their names ("make it a medium", "extra large please" for M and XL), one word of a longer option ("in green" for Forest green, "do you have it in navy"), delivery by what it does ("next day delivery", "I want it tomorrow", "I'll pick it up in store"), countries by their short names ("I live in the UK", "ship it to the US", "America"), quantities as words ("I'll take two", "just one"), and "go one size up" from the size chosen. A choice can also be said by its rank: "the largest size" or "the smallest one" by size, "the cheapest" or "the most expensive" by the prices in the options, "the quickest delivery" by the delivery times they state (same day, next day, 3-5 days), "the first option" or "the last one" in the only dropdown there; radio buttons are ranked the same way. A rank that fits two dropdowns and names neither, or one whose options cannot be ranked so ("the cheapest size"), is not guessed. A choice can also be said as a preference ("softback is fine", "a small would be better", "the biggest one you have", "the fastest way to get it", "I'll pick it up from the shop") or as what a message is about ("I have a complaint about my order" for Order problem, "I've got an idea for a book" for Book suggestion), with other words for common option words (softback and hardback for Paperback and Hardcover, shop for store, complaint for problem, idea for suggestion); radio buttons in the circle or just outside it are chosen the same way. In a basket, "three hats please" sets the hat's quantity. Every meaningful word said must belong to the one option chosen: two such options are asked about (including an option named exactly another way, such as L for "large", beside one only containing the word), and a choice the page does not offer ("in pink", "size 12", "two-day delivery") does nothing. On a held-out set of 32 such instructions on a clothes shop's dropdowns, written before these rules by the same developer, 32 were right and none acted wrongly (10 before); an earlier held-out set rose from 39 to 41 of 50 and the others were unchanged, with the main suite at 120 of 120.
- **Controls repeated on every row**: a basket's Remove, Quantity and Save for later, or an address book's Edit and Delete, are reached by what their row says: "remove the socks", "take the hat out of my basket", "I don't want the mug any more", "save the hat for later", "make the mug two", "I want three hats", "three hats please", "edit my work address", "update the address at work", "delete mum's address". Each control is captured with the text of the list item, table row or small card it sits in. Only a row mentioning every meaningful word said is chosen, two such rows are asked about by their text, and asking to remove, save or set the quantity of something no row mentions does nothing. On a held-out set of 26 instructions on a basket and an address book, written before these rules, the interpreter went from 8 right to 26, with none acting wrongly.
- **Menus that open under the pointer**: "show the Shop menu", "hover over Shop" or "point at Shop" opens a site's hover menu without following the Shop link: the control is focused and given the events a mouse sends on its way in, then the reply says how many more controls appeared, so "open gift cards" can follow. Pointing at another menu leaves the first, and "close the menu" leaves it. A menu drawn only by the page's own CSS `:hover` style cannot be opened this way, and the reply says to click it or use the pointer. A control with an open and closed state (a menu button) is expanded as before.
- **Double-click and right-click**: "double-click Trip notes" (or "this") sends a mouse's presses, clicks and double-click, as file lists and editors expect; a control that may have consequences is confirmed first. "Right-click Photos" or "open the menu for Photos" opens a page's own right-click menu, whose items can then be named; where a page has none, the reply says so, since the browser's own right-click menu cannot be opened by voice.
- **Topic words**: everyday words for about 40 common website topics are understood without the model, so "take me to the stuff about cars" opens GOV.UK's "Driving and transport" and "open careers" finds "Working, jobs and pensions". A related word counts for less than a word the control actually says. A control found only through its topic is used when it is inside the circle, and offered for confirmation when it is just outside. Words that are common in unrelated link text ("learn", "show", "policy", "pay") are never treated as related.
- **Pointing recovery**: a control named in an instruction but not in the circle is looked for just outside it (up to three circle-radii), where it is used and the reply says so, and then anywhere on screen. A far control is only outlined and offered ("“Log in” is not near your gaze… Say confirm"); two or three far matches become a numbered question, and more are refused. Unnamed requests such as "click this" never reach outside the circle.
- **Continuous dictation**: say “start dictation into Message” (or “start dictating” while looking at one field), then speak phrases without a command prefix. A persistent indicator names the field. Punctuation and paragraphs work, and “undo typing” takes back one phrase. Page commands such as “click Send” are literal text in this mode; “help with the navigator” gives help without typing it. Say “stop dictation” to return to commands. Pause, cancel, stopping listening, tracking loss or changing the page focus ends the mode. Protected or locked fields are refused. Rich-text formatting stays in place; continuous dictation in frames from other sites is not supported, so use ordinary “type…” commands there.
- **Dictation and correction** in text fields and rich-text editors (mail and comment composers): type, replace a word, delete the last word, select text or a specific occurrence, insert at the cursor, replace or delete the selection, and undo per field. Editors keep their formatting and links. An editor's toolbar is used as people say it: "insert a link", "add a picture", "attach a file", "make it bold", "italics", "underline that", "bullet points" or "start a numbered list" press the toolbar control that does it (the words are typed only when no such control is in or just outside the circle), and "select all" uses a page's Select all control when one is there. A correction such as "change Turnig to Turing" reaches the field being typed in even after the gaze drifts off it, when the words are in that field. Explicit **"continue typing …"**, **"keep typing …"** or **"continue writing …"** append words to the focused regular text field even when gaze rests on another field; ordinary "type …" keeps its gaze-based target. **"Undo typing"** can recover that focused field when there is no text field in the circle. Text edits recheck website focus handlers before changing text or Undo history: changed values, types or protection, locks, disconnection and redirected focus are refused. Continuation also requires the same page focus; the navigator’s own instruction box preserves the last page focus. Name the first field explicitly with “type hello into Message” or “type hello in the Message field/box/input”: nearby fields are recovered and distant fields need confirmation. A missing named field leaves other fields unchanged. Quote the whole dictated phrase to keep words such as “meet in the football field” literal. Spoken punctuation is typed as marks, as in dictation software: “hello comma how are you question mark” types “hello, how are you?”, with full stop or period, exclamation mark, colon, semicolon, ellipsis, open/close bracket and quote, new line and new paragraph, and the word after a sentence's end or a new line starts with a capital (quote a phrase to keep such words). Text of a clear kind finds its field: an email address, a phone number, a UK postcode or a street address (“12 High Street”) goes to the one field in the circle asking for it, even just after another field was filled, and a spelled-out address (“jo at example dot com”) is written as jo@example.com in an email field. When the gaze rests on a field asking for something else, the right field just outside the circle is offered for confirmation. Boxes for free text keep everything dictated into them.
- **Native form controls**: dropdowns, adding or removing multi-select options, checkboxes and switches, radios, website tabs, expand/collapse disclosures, sliders, and number, date, time, month and week fields. Values can be spoken naturally ("set departure to November first", "set appointment time to 2:30 pm", "set guests to four") or given as digits. A date asked for in separate Day, Month and Year boxes (as on GOV.UK and many bank forms) is filled from one spoken date ("type the twelfth of March twenty nineteen"). Entry stops if the instruction or boxes change; any partial date stays visible for checking. A dropdown can be set by its own name and the choice ("sort by most views"). Value commands have their own undo. Read-only, disabled and changed controls are refused rather than forced.
- **"Again"** ("do that again", "once more", "repeat") repeats the last scroll in the same place, going back or forward, a zoom step, or a press of an ordinary control still on the page, for up to two minutes. Typing and consequential presses are never repeated this way.
- **Find words on the page**: "find gift cards on this page", "look on the page for returns" or "search this page for free delivery" scrolls to the first place the words appear on or below the top of the screen (visible text only, case and accents aside) and outlines it; "next match" and "previous match" step through the others, and "clear the search" removes the outline. When the words are in a link or button, the reply names it and "click it" uses it. "Find" or "look for" without naming the page still uses the site's own search box.
- **Keys by voice**: "press escape", "press tab", "press shift tab", "press page down", "press the down arrow", "press control enter" or "press command a" send that key to the page's focused element, without the gaze. Pages see them as key events, so Escape closes dialogs that listen for it; since the browser itself does nothing with such keys, Tab and Shift+Tab move the focus and paging keys scroll when the page leaves them alone. A key named like a nearby control ("press home" by a Home link) is that control unless said as a key ("press the home key"). Control or Command with Enter, which often sends, is asked first; "press enter" alone still submits the field being typed in. If the focused page target changes before confirmation, give the shortcut again.
- **Videos and sounds by voice**: "play", "pause the video", "mute", "unmute", "louder", "volume 40 percent", "skip ahead 30 seconds", "go back 10 seconds", "jump to 1:30", "restart", "speed up", "play at 1.5 times speed" and "captions on" control the video or sound under the circle, else the one playing, else the only one on the page, including hidden players that music and podcast sites drive from their own buttons. Players often show their buttons only while a mouse is over them, so the media element itself is used. Bare words such as "play" and "mute" count only when the page has a video and no control near the gaze is named like them (a game's Play button, a call's Mute button), "pause" alone still pauses the navigator (the reply points out a video still playing), and "go back" alone still goes back a page. When the browser refuses sound because the page has not been clicked or typed in, the video plays muted and the reply says why. "Again" repeats a skip, volume or speed step. Videos inside players embedded from other sites are not reached; their own buttons can be clicked.
- **Cookie banners and pop-ups by name**: "accept cookies", "reject cookies" (or "only necessary cookies", or the kind a banner names: "reject additional cookies", "accept analytics cookies"), "cookie settings" and "close the pop-up" press that button on the page's cookie banner or pop-up wherever it is, without looking at it, including banners in open shadow roots and same-origin frames. A banner is a dialog, a fixed or sticky layer, or a container named for consent, whose text mentions cookies or consent. Naming the choice is the choice, so a consent button is pressed without the usual confirmation. "Reject" prefers "Reject all" and checks reject wording before accept (so "Accept only necessary" rejects); a banner without a reject button on its first page says to open its settings. Banners inside frames from other sites are not reached.
- **Forms from saved details**: "remember my name is Jo Smith", "remember my email is jo at example dot com" (and phone number, street address, town or city, postcode) keep those details on this device. On a form, "fill in my details" (or "fill in my email", "fill in this form") fills the empty fields of the form nearest your gaze that ask for them, matched by their autocomplete hint, type, label and name: a first or last name comes from your full name. It says which fields it will fill and waits for confirm; "undo" straight afterwards empties them again. Fields that already hold something, passwords, payment and one-time codes, and free-text boxes are left alone. "What details do you have" lists what is saved without reading it out, and "forget my phone number" or "forget my details" deletes them.
- **Numbers on every control**: "show numbers" puts a number on every control on the screen (up to 150, in reading order), and saying one ("click 12", or just "12", in digits or words) uses that control wherever it is, without looking at it, with the usual confirmation for consequential ones. This works while camera tracking is lost too, but not during calibration. A control keeps its number while it stays on screen as the page scrolls or changes, so a number cannot change while it is being said; a "Which one?" question still takes its own numbers, and "hide numbers" removes them. Controls in embedded frames from other sites are not numbered.
- **The browser by voice**: "reload", "go to bbc dot co dot uk" (a website said by its address, ending in a country code or a common ending such as .com, so files and names with dots are not taken for one), "open wikipedia dot org in a new tab", "next tab", "previous tab", "close this tab" (asked first), "zoom in", "zoom out", "reset zoom", "top of the page" and "bottom of the page". A control on the page named like the address is opened instead. With access to all websites, the navigator carries on in the page or tab the command leads to and stops in the tab left behind; without it, the reply says how to turn it back on. Tabs and zoom need the extension; on the practice site, reload and websites work.
- **"It" means the control just used**: for 30 seconds after an action, while that control is still on the page, "it" refers back to it, so "tick Lettuce" then "untick it" works even after the gaze drifts to a neighbouring checkbox. "This" and "that" still mean what is in the gaze circle.
- **Custom widgets** built by component libraries: "set size to large" opens a custom combobox, types into it first when it is a search box, waits for its suggestions and picks the one named choice, then checks the combobox shows it. A long list that puts only the choices in view on the page (a virtualized list) is scrolled through from the top until the named one appears, an exact name before one that only contains it. Several or no matches name the choices and leave the list open. Lists that take several choices (native multi-selects, listboxes marked as such, and comboboxes that show the choices made as chips, as MUI and react-select do) are added to and taken off in everyday words: "choose jazz", "add the godfather and pulp fiction to favorites", "remove inception", "deselect leather seats", "take red off". A choice made already is never clicked off by choosing it again; a new one is checked by its chip or its option's state, and a chip is taken off with its remove button, its delete icon or Backspace. Custom sliders and spinbuttons are moved with their arrow, Home and End keys to the exact spoken value; steps that skip it, ranges and unresponsive widgets are reported. Tree items open and close with their arrow keys ("expand projects"), switches turn on and off ("turn on notifications", "enable dark mode"), and keys carry the legacy key codes older widgets still read.
- **Circle recovery**: say "make the circle bigger/smaller" or "use a small/medium/large circle" to select the existing 60/90/130 px radii. The size is saved and the next page instruction uses the new area. Resizing retires pending choices/confirmations and never activates a control; it also works while paused without resuming.
- **Safety**: equally plausible targets get a numbered "Which one?" question. A control inside the circle that shares only some of the words said gives way to one named closely just outside it, and one named closely further away is offered alongside it instead of the loose match being used. Page badges and spoken choices use the same numbers. Answer within 30 seconds or give the instruction again. Submits, downloads and controls named like delete, buy, book, send, reply, share, upload, archive or sign out need **confirm**, which expires after 15 seconds. Speech that is not understood leaves a pending confirmation or question waiting without extending its deadline, and a number on its own only answers a question ("click 2" activates). "Pause", "cancel" and "stop" act on the interim transcript; "cancel" never presses a website button, and says to use "click cancel" when one is in view. Links with non-web schemes such as `javascript:` are never opened. "Undo" (or "take that back", "change it back") within two minutes of ticking a box, opening or closing a section, choosing from a dropdown or picking a radio button puts the control back as it was, through the same checks as any change; typing done since is undone first. A confirmed click or double-click is refused if the control’s label or native destination changed before it starts. Use a single click for a consequential link or submit button. If a page changes a control during a confirmed double-click, the remaining events stop and the navigator says whether a click was already sent. A confirmed submit also checks that the form and its native destination have not changed. In an embedded frame, looking at another control while waiting for confirmation does not change the original target.
- **Smarter target matching (optional)**: on-device [WaterSheep](https://github.com/SamratDuttaOfficial/WaterSheep) runs through ONNX Runtime Web. The rules answer first. The model is asked only when the rules cannot name a control: first about the controls in the circle, then, if none is meant, about those just outside it. It sees control roles and names, never field values or page text.
- **Panel placement**: the panel stays above website layers whatever their z-index, and moves to another corner when it would hide a website's fixed controls, such as a cookie banner. The page's ordinary controls under a corner count too, at half weight, so it does not leave a "Back to top" button to cover the page's own form. It avoids corners that would cover where you are looking. It can also be changed by voice, since the people it is for may not reach its buttons: "hide the navigator" shrinks it to its answers and any open question, "show the navigator" brings it back, and "move the navigator to the top left" (any corner, or up, down, left and right) keeps it there until "place the navigator automatically". These choices are saved like the circle size and keep an open question.
- **Practice site** (`practice-site/`): a fictional bookshop with no gaze annotations, where the navigator runs without the extension. **Practice tasks**, started from its home page, walk through eight everyday tasks (open a page, sort, search, choose a format, write a note, add to cart, scroll reviews, pick a contact topic). The page checks each task itself and counts the time, the instructions and how the navigator answered them, and actions nobody asked for (detours, orders, removals, other books in the cart). The run stays in the tab, and its JSON report is downloaded only on request, ready for sessions with real users. There is also an **extension setup guide** (`extension-setup.html`).
- **Session log (opt-in)**, for trying the navigator with real users on real sites. Turned on in the extension's toolbar popup (on the practice website, in the panel's settings), it keeps each instruction's time, website, whether it was spoken or typed, the pointer (mouse, eyes or head), what happened (acted, asked, cancelled, failed or other) and how long the answer took. What was said and the replies are kept only when that is turned on too. **Download log** saves a JSON file with totals in all and per website; clearing takes a second press.
- **Trial tasks**, for those sessions. In the extension's toolbar popup, write the tasks one per line and press **Start trial** (it turns the session log on). The panel shows the task at hand on every website and tab; the person trying it says "task done" or "skip task" to move on, or "what's my task" to hear it again, and each instruction in the log notes its task. The downloaded log then reports each task's outcome, time and how its instructions went. **End trial** stops it early; the tasks stay in the popup for the next person. Real websites cannot check a task themselves, as the practice tasks do, so the person says when it is done.
- **Dwell lab** (`index.html`): WebGazer dwell selection in a practice inbox, with nearby-target assist, a gaze keyboard, session drafts with JSON backup, and a held-out accuracy check whose measurement you can download.
- **Decision lab** (`decision-lab.html`): compares a geometric nearby-target baseline with an optional local Ollama model on a synthetic task suite, with JSON report import and export.

## Known gaps

- The real end-to-end loop has not been evaluated with intended users (the practice tasks, the session log and trial tasks are the intended way to start): physical webcam gaze plus a real microphone on representative sites. Each calibration now measures its own accuracy and keeps learning from used controls, but drift, latency and calibration continuity across real users have not been measured. The five-second inactivity check detects missing estimates, and a camera picture that stays exactly the same for three seconds (a frozen camera still produces estimates) is treated the same way until it moves again; neither timeout has been evaluated with real users or real camera faults.
- Camera gaze was measured offline on MPIIFaceGaze, laptop webcam pictures of 15 people (used for evaluation only, not redistributed). The estimator was calibrated on 27 frames near the nine calibration points and tested on 40 other frames from the same day. WebGazer's own estimate (a regression on eye-patch pixels) landed a median of 81 mm from the looked-at point (171 mm at the 90th percentile). That is barely better than always guessing the middle of the calibration points (90 mm), and the estimator fits its own calibration frames to within 0.2 mm, so it overfits them. These were single frames without smoothing or learning from used controls, across a day's changes in posture and light, so use within one sitting should do better. Even so, the 120 px (about 25 mm) error the scripted evaluations simulate is optimistic. The iris estimate the camera mode now uses landed a median of 58 mm (133 mm at the 90th percentile), and was closer for 13 of the 15 people. Calibrated on one day and tested on another, it landed 68 mm; topped up with the frames near five dots from the later day, 57 mm, as close as that day's own nine-dot calibration, which is why a refresh adds five dots instead of starting over. Replaying the later day's 67 frames as a session of looks at controls, in random order, the error over the session's second half was 70 mm without learning from used controls, 68 mm learning only from those within one and a half radii of the gaze (as the navigator did), and 60 mm learning from those just outside the circle too (63 mm when a fifth of them were not what the eyes were on). The iris estimate has not yet been tried live with a real camera.
- On real sites, the pointer has stood in for gaze and typed text for speech so far. With simulated gaze error as large as measured for webcams (220 px each way, so a look lands a median of about 260 px from the control) and the large circle the accuracy check then recommends, 130 of 132 task trials completed (44 tasks, each three times, each trial in a fresh browser profile), with no unintended actions; in the other two the script found nothing to look at (Wikipedia's search box, which it has since learned to follow, and a Hacker News page served without its More link). On the first 37 tasks with 120 px of error, 110 of 111 completed. The tasks include custom radio groups, tabs, accordions, comboboxes, sliders, menu buttons, switches, listboxes, tree views, a modal dialog, spin buttons and a toolbar from the WAI-ARIA examples and MUI, GOV.UK Design System form questions, a shop (Books to Scrape), a log-in form, Hacker News and BBC News, named as people say them ("choose thin crust", "show the tab for Ida da Fonseca"). In MUI's autocomplete that takes several films, adding two by name and taking one off completed 6 of 6 trials at that error, with no unintended actions; the list was confirmed first when the gaze landed far from it. The last four found two faults, since fixed: a password box whose label pointed at the username box by mistake was missed, so the password went into the username (a field without a name of its own is now called by the label just before it, or a password box "Password"), and on a page as crowded with links as Hacker News a link named inside the circle, but beyond its nearest dozen controls, was not found. The navigator panel can cover controls in its corner. Camera gaze that lands within the circle's radius inside a panel edge facing the page looks at the page just outside that edge (tested with a stand-in camera); deeper inside, the circle stops on the last page area. A control under the panel still counts as on the page, so it is found by name like any other and pressed though you cannot see it, and before the gaze has rested on the page at all a control said by name is looked for anywhere on screen and offered. The pointer standing in for gaze in these tasks keeps that last area anywhere over the panel, as a real pointer should, so they do not measure the edge. Everyday names for common page tools are understood ("past versions" for History, "basket" for Cart, "preferences" for Settings), and so are everyday words for common topics: "the stuff about cars" now reaches GOV.UK's Driving section in every trial, where the rules alone found it in none. Paraphrases outside the topic vocabulary still need the optional model, and remaining misses include scroll regions far from the gaze. Typing without naming a field after a large gaze miss ("type hello" with no field in the circle) offers the nearest text field just outside it, or the only one to three on screen, and text of a clear kind ("12 High Street", an email address) the one field asking for it wherever it is; it types only after you confirm or pick a number; "press enter" submits the field being typed in even after the gaze drifts. The scripted checkbox task uses the WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices example, because Wikipedia's log-in page refuses automated browsers.
- WaterSheep matching is opt-in and only chooses targets. It needs a model download of about 158 MB on first use. The rule interpreter still misses many paraphrases; those end in a question or an explanation, not an action. Rules carry little to wording they were not written for, which is why requests not understood now offer the control their words point to: of 32 everyday requests written after choices could be said as a preference, by quantity per item and as a wish to tick a box, and scored once, the rules understood 15 (47%), exactly as many as before those rules, and 8 more were offered correctly for a yes; one offer was of the wrong size (XS for "a small would be better", since fixed) and none acted wrongly. Rules written for one set's misses barely carry to new wording, so understanding it more widely probably needs a language model rather than more rules.
- Custom widgets are driven through their documented keyboard and pointer behaviour, so a widget that ignores both may not respond as expected. A list that takes several choices is checked by its chips or its options' state, so one showing the choices made some other way is not; several such lists in the circle holding the choice named are asked about, so name the list on pages that repeat one. Looking through a virtualized list stops after eight seconds. Embedded frames from other sites need the extension's all-websites access, and their controls are outlined where they were when captured.
- Scroll recovery samples visible points inside the circle; a tiny region between samples may need a more direct look. Unnamed requests ("scroll down") do not reach distant regions; named ones can, by the region's markup (lists, sidebars, menus, comments, chat, results, tables) or by its label or heading; an area with neither, or whose heading is not right before it, still needs a direct look. A bounded search of semantic containers supplements the viewport grid for thin panes; very large pages or unmarked regions can still require a direct look. Covered or fully clipped panes are not discovery candidates.
- Spoken commands are English only. Recognition uses your browser’s primary language when it is English, otherwise the first English language in its preferences, or US English if none is listed. That choice stays the same for recognition, on-device availability and installation until the navigator restarts. Without on-device recognition, Chrome's speech recognition depends on the browser vendor's service. On-device recognition and its favouring of control names have been tested only with stand-in recognizers, not real speech. Synthetic speech is no stand-in for a microphone: the task commands spoken by Piper voices and recognised by Parakeet were misheard about a third of the time even without noise, so it served only to find kinds of mishearing to recover from. Chrome may require installing the language pack from its own settings if the download offered here is refused.
- Chrome is the only supported browser. There is no store listing; install the unpacked build. Protected pages (`chrome://`, the Web Store) cannot be controlled.
- The decision lab's Ollama path is a separate experiment and is not connected to live navigation.

## Run locally

Prerequisites: Node.js 20 or newer (CI uses 24), npm, Python 3 (for the static server) and Chrome 116+.

```sh
cd apps/gaze-navigator
npm ci --ignore-scripts
python3 -m http.server 4173
```

Open `http://localhost:4173` for the landing page, dwell lab and links to the labs. The practice site is at `http://localhost:4173/practice-site/index.html`. Camera APIs need localhost or HTTPS.

**Extension.** `npm run build:extension` writes `dist/extension`. It fetches WebGazer 3.3.0 and the WaterSheep browser runtime with npm and checks each against a pinned SHA-256. Then open `chrome://extensions`, enable **Developer mode**, choose **Load unpacked** and select `apps/gaze-navigator/dist/extension`. Review shortcuts at `chrome://extensions/shortcuts`. Leave the extension off on the practice site, or you will get two panels.

**WaterSheep (smarter matching).**
- *Extension:* the runtime is bundled. The model downloads from Hugging Face the first time you enable the setting. It is pinned to a revision and checked by SHA-256.
- *Practice site:* run `npm run vendor:watersheep` once. This writes the runtime to `dist/vendor/watersheep`, then enable **Smarter target matching** under **Tracking and settings**.
- *Developer evaluation:* `npm run evaluate:instructions -- --watersheep DIR` scores a local model copy against the instruction suites. It needs `npm install --no-save onnxruntime-node@1.23.0 @huggingface/tokenizers@0.2.0`.

**Decision lab with a local model (optional).** Install Ollama 0.35+ yourself and run it local-only:

```sh
OLLAMA_NO_CLOUD=1 OLLAMA_HOST=127.0.0.1:11434 ollama serve
ollama pull tev1:0.8b-q8_0        # about 812 MB; any GGUF decision model works
npm run benchmark:local -- --model tev1:0.8b-q8_0 > decision-report.json
```

The lab talks only to `127.0.0.1:11434` and refuses to run unless the server reports cloud disabled. The app never downloads models. An example report for import is at `browser-tests/fixtures/tev1-report.json`.

## Verify

```sh
npm test                 # Node unit tests (node --test)
npm run lint             # syntax check of every JavaScript file
npm run build            # lint + test build of the extension (dist/extension-test)
npx playwright install chromium
npm run test:browser     # Playwright: dwell lab, practice site, decision lab, extension
```

Browser tests start `python3 -m http.server` on port 4173 themselves. They use a fake speech recognizer and a fake gaze estimator, so they check interface behaviour, not webcam accuracy. CI: `.github/workflows/gaze-navigator.yml`. The manual-dispatch `.github/workflows/gaze-decision-benchmark.yml` runs the Ollama benchmark. `node scripts/evaluate-real-sites.mjs --extension dist/extension-test [--assist] [--gaze-error 120 --trials 3]` drives a built extension through scripted tasks on live public sites; `--gaze-error` adds webcam-like pointing error (standard deviation in pixels) and reports completion, unintended actions and questions. Instructions are typed in keyboard command mode, which like speech leaves the website's focus in place (so menus and dialogs stay open); `--panel-typing` types into the panel instead.

## Data & privacy

- Password, one-time-code and payment-card fields stay writable, but their values are never shown in the panel, announced, spoken or sent to the WaterSheep model.
- Embedded frames answer only through the extension: the navigator in the top page receives their controls' roles, names, positions and non-sensitive values, and sends back confirmed actions. Neither page's scripts can read or send these messages; a frame is identified by a random number the navigator posts into it.
- The navigator never records, uploads or stores audio or page content, and keeps what was said only in a session log you turn on with what was said, and in the requests it learned from your yes to an offer and the phrases you taught it (below). The extension's session log stays in its local storage, written by pages but read, downloaded and cleared only in the toolbar popup, which websites cannot reach; on the practice website it lasts as long as the tab. In Chrome and Edge, the browser's own speech recognition sends microphone audio to the browser vendor. Typed instructions work without a microphone.
- In the extension, the camera and WebGazer run in an invisible extension frame (`extension/capture.html`), so camera access is granted to Gaze Navigator once (through its setup tab), not to each website, and pages cannot see the video. Only gaze points reach the page's content script, over a private extension port.
- Speech recognition runs in a separate extension capture frame, so its microphone permission belongs to Gaze Navigator. The setup tab requests audio only after you click Allow microphone, then stops that check immediately. Stop listening, leaving the page or turning the navigator off destroys the listening frame. Native microphone permission prompts and real speech still need physical verification; automated tests use a controlled recognizer. A website’s restrictive permissions policy may prevent capture; typed instructions remain available.
- Webcam frames are processed in the browser by WebGazer and are not uploaded or stored. The extension bundles WebGazer and its face models (pinned by SHA-256 at build time), and its camera frame makes no network requests at all. On the practice website, WebGazer loads from jsDelivr and its face models from tfhub.dev.
- Extension state lives in extension storage, never in the website's storage. Navigator settings and camera calibration samples (small eye-patch images plus screen positions) are kept in in-memory session storage. "Stop camera" deletes the samples, and closing the browser discards them. The popup's stay-on and auto-start preferences are kept in extension local storage. The website version keeps settings in `sessionStorage` and recalibrates on every page.
- Saved details ("remember my email is …") stay on this device, in the extension's local storage, which websites cannot read; on the practice website they last only as long as the page. They are written into a form only after you confirm, never sent to the WaterSheep model, and the navigator's replies name which details and fields, not their values. "Forget my details" deletes them.
- Learned requests (the words of a spoken request you confirmed, and the name of the control it meant) and taught phrases (the phrase and the instruction it stands for) stay on this device in the extension's local storage, which websites cannot read, until you say "forget what you learned" or forget them one at a time; on the practice website they last for the tab's session. Typed requests are never learned, and phrases cannot be taught by typing.
- The WaterSheep model is cached in Cache Storage: the extension's own cache, or the practice site's origin.
- The navigator runs in the extension's isolated world. Its modules are web-accessible, so a website can detect that the extension is installed.
- Dwell-lab drafts live in memory and are cleared on refresh. Backups and measurements are saved only when you download them.
