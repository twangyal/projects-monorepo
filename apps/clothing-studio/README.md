# Clothing Studio

A browser garment-design workspace. It is for designers who want to go from a template to a styled 3D garment, linked pattern pieces, a tech pack and a costed range. You pick a base product, style each part in real-time 3D, adjust measurements and construction, drape garments on an approximate body, and export renders, 3D models, patterns and spreadsheets. It runs entirely in the browser: no account, backend or paid service.

**Status:** ACTIVE — portfolio priority. Direction: [docs/PRODUCT_DIRECTION.md](../../docs/PRODUCT_DIRECTION.md)

## What works today

- **3D-first workspace.** The editable garment is the main screen. Focused tool sections cover Templates, Materials & prints, Artwork, Patterns & measurements, Body & drape, and Exports. Secondary dialogs open the tech pack, range plan, concept sketch and skirt construction. It works on desktop and phone.
- **Base products and templates**: T-shirt (Blank tee, Breton stripe, Ringer tee, Garden print), Polo shirt, Oxford shirt, Wool coat, Blazer, Tank top, Crop top, T-shirt dress, Fit-and-flare dress, Crew sweatshirt, Sweatshirt dress, Bomber jacket, Pullover hoodie, Zip hoodie, Pull-on shorts, Straight trousers, Jeans, Joggers, Flared skirt, Gathered skirt, Pleated skirt and Pencil skirt. A top 52 cm long or less (down to 40 cm) ends above the waist and is named a crop top (or a cropped sweatshirt or hoodie); one long enough to pass the hips (85 cm or more, up to 120 cm) is a dress: it drapes over the hips and thighs and is named as a dress in the tech pack and patterns. Modules switch hem shape, sleeve length and a chest pocket. **Polo collar and placket** turns a round-necked T-shirt into a polo: a flat-knit collar replaces the neck rib, cut as a rectangle as long as the neckline between the placket's edges, and a three-button placket runs down the centre front. In 3D the collar stands about 2 cm and folds down, its points lying on the chest either side of the placket; the tech pack gives the collar height, placket length and how both are sewn, and "polo" in a description does the same. **Button front** turns a T-shirt into a shirt: the front is cut as mirrored halves joined by a buttoned placket from the neckline to the hem (a button about every 9.5 cm), which **Worn open** parts. With the collar, which then reads **Shirt collar**, it is a button-up shirt (or a short-sleeved shirt or shirt dress), its collar cut in the shirt's own fabric and, being woven, shaped with a wider fall so it lies down; without it, a collarless shirt. **Coat** makes such a shirt, at least 70 cm long with long sleeves, a coat: it goes over a T-shirt, shirt, polo or dress, and its tech pack gives it larger coat buttons, blind-stitched hems and bound seams (it is unlined). The Wool coat template is a knee-length one in wool twill, and "coat" or "overcoat" in a description turns a top into one, keeping its styling. **Lapels** open a coat in a V above its top button, at the waist: each front folds back along a roll line from there to the neckline into a lapel 8 cm wide at its point by default, lying on the chest, rolled along the fold and edge-stitched, and styled on its own; the collar runs round the back neck between the roll lines, meeting each lapel with a notch, and the coat buttons below without a placket, its buttons 10 cm apart (two on a hip-length coat, which is then named a blazer). The pattern cuts each lapel on its front, marking the roll line as a fold, with a front facing (the piece styled as the lapels) in place of the placket, and the tech pack gives the break point and lapel width and how the lapels and collar are sewn. The Blazer template is a hip-length navy wool one; "blazer", "sport coat" or "suit jacket" in a description turns a top into one, "notched lapels" adds them to a coat, "black lapels" styles them and "no lapels" takes them off. **Peak lapels** instead point each lapel up above where the collar meets it, 9 cm wide at the point by default, in 3D, the pattern and the tech pack, which sets the collar under each peak; "peak lapels" in a description makes them, and "notched lapels" or "no peak lapels" makes them notched again. **Lapel width** in Fit changes either style from 4 to 12 cm, measured square from the roll line at the point, reshaping the actual front and facing in the pattern and 3D garment; the tech pack and graded sizes retain that width. Authored widths must keep the point at least 0.5 cm inside the shoulder, a peak above its collar join, and the front and facing free of crossing edges. A description such as "blazer, lapel width 6 cm" applies the same edit. Taking lapels off keeps their width for when they are restored; Back to proportional clears it. **Lined** lines a coat's body and sleeves in viscose lining in the body's colour, styled on its own as the Lining and shown inside the 3D garment; the pattern adds the lining's fronts (behind the facings, or to the front edge behind a placket), back (with a 2 cm centre-back pleat) and sleeves, each ending 2 cm above the hem it is pleated over, and the tech pack buys the lining fabric and bags the lining in instead of binding the seams. "Lined", "a red lining" and "unlined" in a description do the same. "Shirt", "button-up", "oxford" and "shirt dress" in a description do the same. **Back yoke** cuts a shirt's back across 9 cm below the neck into a yoke over the shoulders (cut double, the outer yoke and its facing) and the back body, sewn together in 3D and styled on their own; "navy yoke" or "contrast yoke" in a description adds one in that colour, and "no yoke" takes it off. **Shirt cuffs** end a long-sleeved shirt's sleeves (45 cm or longer) in 6 cm buttoned cuffs styled on their own: each sleeve stops 6 cm short, cut 4 cm wider to be pleated in, and each cuff is cut on the fold with a 2 cm overlap for its button; "white cuffs" or "contrast cuffs" in a description adds them, "no cuffs" takes them off, and shortening the sleeves removes them. On a sweatshirt the same checkbox reads **Rib stand collar**: a rib collar, cut on the fold, stands 6 cm from a round neck instead of the neck rib (a funnel-neck sweatshirt). With **Front zip**, which sweatshirts now take as hoodies do, it is a **bomber jacket**; the template is cut in nylon poplin with rib cuffs, hem band and collar. The collar and neck rib run from one side of the zip round the back to the other, and part with the front halves when worn open. "Bomber", "flight jacket", "track jacket" or "jacket", "zip-up" and "funnel neck" in a description do the same. A T-shirt with a sleeve length of 0 (the Sleeveless preset) is a tank top: no sleeve pieces, its armholes bound and left open, and named as a tank top (or tank dress) in the tech pack. A skirt's waist can be gathered (1.1 to 2 times the finished waist, with the waist edge kept at least 10 cm shorter than the hem): the panels are cut with the longer waist edge, the drape draws it in so the extra fabric falls into gathers, and the tech pack says how far to gather it. A skirt can instead be **pleated**, with 8 to 40 knife pleats (all folded the same way) or box pleats (pairs folded away from each other), each 1 to 6 cm deep at the waist and deeper toward the hem as the skirt flares: the panels are cut wider by the folded underlays, the patterns mark each pleat's fold line and the placement line it is folded to (SVG, DXF and the tech pack diagram), the tech pack gives the pleats' spacing and depth at waist and hem, and the side seams fall inside the pleats. In 3D the folds are pressed creases: every layer is sewn into the waist seam, the underlays stay inside the fabric over them, and the folds open a little toward the hem rather than lying flat; the opening angle is a set value, not measured from fabric. "Pleated", "box pleats" and "no pleats" in a description do the same, and wool twill joins the fabric library. A skirt given a **fitted hip** is fitted to the hip 20 cm below the waist instead of flaring: its panels' side seams curve in above the hip and waist darts take in the rest (two a panel, four when two would each be wider than 3 cm), its waist-to-hem slant becomes its length, and a hem narrower than the hip makes a pencil skirt (named so; as wide as the hip, a straight skirt). The patterns mark each dart by its legs (SVG, DXF, the pattern preview and the tech pack diagram), the tech pack gives the darts and the hip, and in 3D each dart's legs are sewn together, closing it; the skirt starts oval like the hips it drapes on, which are its hip less 4 cm. "Pencil", "straight" or "fitted" in a description fit a skirt; "A-line" or "flared" undo it.
- **Part-by-part styling**: drag fabrics, colours and prints onto parts in 3D, or pick a part and click. The library has 11 fabrics with weight and composition, plus 6 print motifs. Repeat layout and size are in real centimetres. You can upload up to 6 of your own repeat motifs, and artwork for the front, the back and each sleeve with size, position and rotation: on tops it is placed from the shoulder, and on shorts, trousers and skirts from the waist seam, new artwork going on the wearer's left hip in front (the right behind) or centred below a skirt's waist. The tech pack says which leg a print on trousers is on, and shorter legs, cuffs or a smaller size move a print up or in rather than refusing the change. **Sleeve artwork** is placed on the sleeve pattern piece, from its centre line and below the shoulder point, so it wraps round the arm and follows the drape; it is printed on the cut sleeve before sewing (as the tech pack says), so it must stay clear of the seams, and the message says where it fits. A shorter or narrower sleeve or a smaller size moves it to stay on, and a top made sleeveless loses it. **Print this text** sets words (up to 40 characters, wrapped at about 20 characters a line, in sans serif, serif or monospace and any colour) as a side's or sleeve's artwork; a description such as `"TEAM" down the left sleeve` runs it down the sleeve. **Pick colours from a photo** (a product shot, mood picture or swatch) reads its main colours on the device, middle of the picture first, and keeps them as swatches with the design (not the photo); **Use them on the garment** puts the main colour on the body and a contrasting one on trims and prints in one undoable step. Artwork can be placed by clicking the 3D garment. **On the pattern pieces** each print shows where it is printed on the cut piece, right side up: laid flat as placed on a top's body and sleeves, and on shorts, trousers and skirts traced through the garment from its straight-on view, so its edges curve and widen where the legs or skirt turn away. A print over the centre front of a front cut in halves, or on the other leg, is marked where it lands with the pattern turned over. A print laid flat can be dragged to a new place on its piece, as one undoable edit that the 3D view and exports follow; one dragged off its piece is refused and goes back.
- **Front pocket bags**: scoop pockets on trousers and shorts include an inner bag matching the front opening and a full outer bag, each cut as a mirrored pair in natural 130 gsm cotton pocketing. The pattern preview and SVG/DXF/tiled/outfit cutting files label both, and the tech pack measures their pocketing separately from the styled facing fabric; its linear conversion is a bulk area equivalent, not a single-garment cutting marker. Their rounded lower paths match; the nominal bag is narrowed to fit the front waist (at close waists it reaches the centre seam) and shortened to stay above the crotch. Grain follows Front pockets. Bags are cutting pieces, not simulated internal cloth; inspect the finish and validate a toile.
- **Fabric grain per part**: set −90° to 90° beside Fabric. Zero follows the upward pattern vertical; ±45° cuts on the bias and 90° across, with positive angles clockwise. The pattern preview, SVG/DXF/tiled cutting sheets and tech packs mark that grain, and the outfit ZIP records each part’s angle. It is saved with styles, colorways and versions, has Undo, and restarts the directional-stretch solve. Print/artwork orientation and finished pattern geometry stay unchanged. Mirrored cuts use their source-pattern grain independently of print UVs. Modelled trims keep their existing response; bias creep and physical fabric calibration remain missing. Consumption still uses an area-based estimate, without a grain-aware cutting marker.
- **Describe a style or a garment**: a local, deterministic text parser (no AI). For one part it reads about 700 colour names (fashion names plus everyday names from the xkcd colour survey, with modifiers such as pale or dusty), fabrics, motifs, stripe direction, repeat size and layout. **Apply to the garment** also reads the garment type (starting from its template; "mini", "knee-length", "midi", "maxi" (or "ankle-length", "long dress") and "floor-length" set a dress's length, up to 150 cm), neckline, sleeve length, hem, chest pocket, a hoodie's front zip ("zip hoodie", "zip-up", or "pullover" for none) and fit ("oversized", "cropped", "wide leg"), a gathered skirt waist, sleeveless and tank tops, crop tops ("cropped" ends a top above the waist), and styles each named part from its own clause ("oversized navy hoodie with a cream hood and white cuffs"). Words it understands but cannot apply to the design are listed with the reason ("pleated skirt" on a dress, "navy skirt" on a dress not cut at the waist). Explicit named dimensions in cm or mm override fit presets: "chest 110 cm, 700 mm length, sleeves 25 cm" for tops, plus shoulder width and drop, neck width, front and back neck drop, armhole depth and flat hem width (half the circumference); waist, hip, rise, inseam, leg opening and ankle cuff height for trousers; waist, hem, slant and waistband height for skirts, plus hip for fitted skirts. Set either band height to 0 to remove it. Invalid or conflicting dimensions refuse the whole description without changing the design. Words in quotes are printed on a top ("black tee with "Stay Wild" on the back in white letters"), in a colour that reads on the garment unless one is named. It reports each change with its scope and lists what it could not apply, including unsupported construction accents such as red topstitching, which do not change the garment colour. One Undo restores the design.
- **Linked measurements and fit**: each sleeve is drafted from its armhole, its cap cut 3% longer than the armhole it is sewn into. **Sleeve cap gathering** (1.2 to 1.7 times the armhole) makes a T-shirt's, shirt's or dress's sleeves puff sleeves: each cap is cut that much longer, spread most at the top, and gathered into the armhole, so the drape puffs it and the tech pack sews it with gathering rows; "puff sleeves" in a description sets 1.5 and "plain sleeves" takes it out. Body measurements, plus points of measure (shoulder, neck, armhole, waist, hem, sleeve opening, shoulder drop, back neck, rib/cuff/hem-band heights, hood size, pocket placement, waistband height). These update the 3D garment, pattern pieces and tech pack together. Pattern handles can be dragged or moved with the keyboard. On a T-shirt, polo, shirt or T-shirt dress, a **waist width** narrower than the chest curves the side seams in through it at the **waist position**, measured down from the high point shoulder (it starts where the default body's waist is): the front and back pieces, the 3D garment and its drape follow the curve, the tech pack adds the waist width (X) and position (Y), graded 2.5 and 0.5 cm a size, and the pattern's waist handles set both. "Fitted waist", "nipped-in waist" or "waisted" in a description take 8 cm off each piece's width at the waist, and "straight side seams" takes the shaping off. Sweatshirts and hoodies hang from their hem band and are not shaped. **Waist seam** cuts a T-shirt dress (85 cm or longer) at its waist line into a bodice and a skirt: each is its own pattern piece and 3D part to style on its own, the drape sews them together where they meet, and the hem width (its handle on the skirt piece) sets the skirt's flare. **Skirt gathering** (1.1 to 2 times the seam) cuts the skirt's waist edge that much longer, easing to the hem, and gathers it into the seam: the pattern, the drape's folds and the tech pack's gathering rows follow. The tech pack sews the waist seam before the side seams and lists a skirt fabric as its own line. **Bust dart intake** (1 to 5 cm) gives a T-shirt base's front (a tee, shirt, coat or dress) a dart at each side seam, 4 cm below the underarm, pointing up toward the bust and stopping 2.5 cm short of it. The front piece is cut that much longer below the darts and its side seams open by the intake where they are, with each dart's legs drawn on the piece and in the SVG/DXF; the 3D front is sewn along the legs, so the extra length reaches over the bust (on a body with a bust the centre front hangs level instead of riding up). The placket, lapel facings and front prints follow the longer front, and the tech pack sews the darts before the side seams and lists the intake (BD). "Bust darts" in a description adds 2.5 cm and "no darts" takes them off. **Waist dart intake** (1 to 4 cm) gives a dress cut at the waist four darts rising from the waist seam: two in the front bodice toward the bust and two in the back toward the shoulder blades. Each bodice is cut that much wider beside each dart, so its side seams are let out and the darts take in the waist instead; the 3D bodice is sewn along the darts' legs, and the tech pack sews them before the waist seam (WD). A top or dress in one piece that reaches at least 8 cm below its waist takes them as darts pointed at both ends, running through the waist to about 14 cm below it (drawn as a V above the waist and one below), sewn point to point before the side seams; taking a dress's waist seam off keeps its darts that way. "Waist darts" (or "fisheye darts") in a description adds 2 cm. "Fit and flare" or "skater dress" in a description shapes the waist, flares the hem and cuts the dress at the waist; "waist seam" and "no waist seam" add or remove it, "gathered skirt", "gathered dress" or "dirndl" gathers the skirt into it one and a half times (widening a narrower hem to match), and "skirt" and "bodice" style either part. Artwork stays on the bodice. A hoodie's or sweatshirt's **Front zip** cuts the front, any kangaroo pocket and the hem band as mirrored halves, shows the zip down the centre front in 3D (the pocket halves stop short of it), and lists the open-end zip and how it is set in the tech pack. **Worn open** parts the front halves and hem band at the zip in 3D, each with its own side of the zip and the slider at the foot, and draped they hang apart (about 20 cm apart at the chest on the default body); the pattern pieces and tech pack stay the same. The hood stays a pullover hood, sewn to the neckline either side of the face opening, so the top of the parting opens wider than on a real zip hoodie. Collar patterns use the exact flat rest coordinates of the 3D collar, including the sampled neck edge and stand/fall fold. Patterns, SVG/DXF and the tech pack show the same draft: cut two upper/under pieces for a woven collar, or one flat-knit piece; changing its fabric updates cutting guidance and estimated fabric use.
- **Drape on a body** for every base: tees, sweatshirts, hoodies, flared skirts, shorts and trousers. Each garment is sewn from its pattern pieces: a top's front and back meet at the shoulder and side seams, its sleeves are set into the armholes as tubes along the arms, and rib cuffs and hem bands, cut 15% shorter, gather the edges they finish. An optional **Body shoulder width** (30–65 cm, straight across the shoulder points) sets its shoulder breadth and arm roots independently of garment measurements, keeping chest, waist and hips fixed; blank keeps automatic proportions. It is carried through body profiles, project backups, version comparisons, graded previews and matched fabric comparisons. With measured shoulders, automatic upper-arm girth follows chest-based proportions; enter upper-arm girth to override it. Shoulder slope and the sleeve-aligned pose remain approximate. An XPBD cloth solver runs in a Web Worker on a measurement-driven body proxy, shown as a smooth mannequin (its torso, arms, neck and head or legs blended into one dress-form surface, drawn just inside the shape the cloth keeps clear of) (chest, optional waist/hip girths and chest depth for tops; waist and hip for skirts, which hang from a held waistline, and for shorts and trousers, whose elastic waistband gathers onto the body waist above two legs). Each fabric's weight, bending rigidity and stretch stiffness change the result: on the same tee and body, knits cling while linen and denim stand off the body with smoother hems. You can compare two fabrics side by side on any garment, skirts and trousers included, and save top body profiles by name. An optional body height sets torso heights, arm length and trouser leg length, and the drape then says where the hem, sleeves and trouser legs end on that body ("the hems would reach 6 cm below the floor"). An optional waist-to-hip depth (5–40 cm) places the fullest hip below the waist on tops, bottoms and outfits, independently of girths and body height; blank keeps the existing proportions. A trouser proxy refuses a hip at or below its crotch. Applied chest, waist, hip, chest depth, height, hip-depth, upper-arm and neck measurements stay with each design through reload, backups, the saved library and Undo/Redo; typing stays as an unapplied draft.
- **Outfits**: in Body & drape, **Wear with** pairs a top with shorts, trousers or a skirt (a template or a saved design), kept with the design as a copy and undoable. The bottoms drape first on the outfit's body, with their waistline at the body's waist, then the top drapes over them, untucked, and the 3D view shows both. **Tuck the top in** reverses the order for a top that is not a dress: the top drapes first, its body (not its sleeves) pressed close to the body below the waistband and then held there, and the bottoms drape over it, a skirt's waist easing out evenly to fit over it. Automatic waist and hip girths are ones both garments fit. The bottoms do not feel the top's weight. **Layers**: a T-shirt (a tee, tank, crop top or T-shirt dress) can also be worn under a sweatshirt or hoodie, the zip hoodie open or closed, or under a button-up shirt, open or buttoned (a shirt itself can go under a sweatshirt or hoodie, but not under another shirt), and any of them, a shirt, a polo or a sweatshirt too (a bomber or sweatshirt dress), under a coat. Both are draped on a body posed for the lower of the two tops' arms (a coat over a sweatshirt hangs from the sweatshirt's lower arms), the one underneath first; the outer top then rests on it as on a thicker body, keeping a little more than the inner top's own stand-off from the body (loose folds count for at most 2.5 cm, and under rib cuffs and bands it is pressed flat), and wherever the outer top lies closer, the one underneath is pressed in under it. It is then kept inside the outer top's body and sleeves, measured out from the torso's and each arm's axis, across its faces as well as at its points, so nothing of it shows in the fold under the arm; it shows only where it would (through an open zip or shirt front, below a hem). Either garment can be the one edited. The top underneath does not feel the outer top's weight, and a top worn over another is not tucked in. **And with** completes such an outfit with a third garment, so a T-shirt, a hoodie and joggers are worn together: the bottoms drape first, the T-shirt over them, then the outer top over both, outside the bottoms as well as the T-shirt. **Garment to edit** lets you jump directly to the inner layer, outer layer or bottoms by name. **Remove only** beside Wear with takes out that garment and keeps the other worn piece; removing both asks for confirmation. Outfit exports include all three, including an outfit built around the initial Blank tee. Exports default to the design on its own; **Include worn garment in PNG and GLB** exports both garments in their settled positions, with their materials, prints and top artwork, without the mannequin. Drape the outfit first and wait for it to finish. The export keeps the captured outfit if you edit or remove a garment while it renders.
- **Sizes in 3D**: in Body & drape, **Size shown** displays and drapes any size from XS to XL, graded from the sample size, on the same body, so the fit of each size can be compared ("15 cm of ease at the chest" for an L where the M has 10). **Apply shown size to the whole outfit** optionally grades every worn piece independently from its own sample to the selected size; otherwise only the edited garment changes. In either mode, the complete body is constructed from the applied samples and stays fixed as clothing sizes change; outfit PNG/GLB/video exports use those graded pieces. Saved designs stay at their sample sizes; individual patterns and outfit ZIP sizes are separate choices. If a later applied edit makes that grade unsupported, the view explicitly returns to the sample and explains the change without discarding the edit. Skirts use +4 cm of waist and +6 cm of hem per size, with side slant and finished band height fixed. These are starting grades to check with a fit sample.
- **Fabric drape test** beside each part's fabric: the garment solver drapes a 30 cm disc of that fabric over an 18 cm support, like a Cusick drape tester, and reports its drape coefficient, its number of folds and its shadow seen from above, next to the range drape testers report for that construction.
- **Material experiments**: tensile, bending and membrane swatch comparisons with SI-unit result downloads. These are illustrative and do not feed the garment solver.
- **Tech pack**: bill of materials with estimated consumption, graded points of measure (XS–XL) with a diagram, each size measured from the design graded to it (5 cm of chest and 1.5 cm of length per size for tops, 5 cm of waist and hip for shorts and trousers; fit points you edited keep their own increments), and construction steps. Exports as Excel (`.xlsx`) or a printable self-contained HTML file.
- **Colorways and range plan**: up to 8 named colorways with renders, plus costing inputs (fabric price per metre, make, trims, print, retail). The range plan shows unit cost, margin and totals, and exports to Excel.
- **Exports**: front/back PNG renders, **studio renders** (1600 × 2000 px PNG from the front, three-quarter, side or back, on a warm studio, white or transparent backdrop: soft light from all round, a key light whose shadows fall across the garment, ambient occlusion in the folds and each fabric's sheen, tone mapped to keep colours true; outfit included if chosen, body never shown), a six-second turntable video (WebM, one turn around the styled or draped garment, outfit included if chosen), GLB 3D model (styled or draped), DXF pattern (AutoCAD R12, cut/sew/grain/fold/artwork/text layers, chosen seam allowance), and a 1:1 printable SVG pattern. Both, the tiled sheets and the outfit ZIP mark each print's edges and name and size on its pieces. **Pattern seam allowance** chooses a uniform 0–2 cm cutting offset (default 1 cm) for SVG, DXF, tiled sheets and every pattern in the outfit ZIP; downloads label it and the bundle manifest records it. This temporary output choice survives workspace edits but returns to 1 cm on reload; it does not change saved measurements, drape, grade selection or costing estimates. Zero cuts on the sewing line. Inspect offset corners and specify hem and closure finishes separately; verify with a toile. **A4 and US Letter tiled HTML** prints every product piece separately at full scale, with one 5 cm calibration square, 1 cm overlap, registration marks and piece/cut-count labels. It uses the selected pattern export size, not the 3D preview size, and refuses product drafts. Open the file, choose its paper, print at 100% without fit-to-page or headers/footers, and check the square before assembly. Exports are bounded to 256 pattern tiles and 2 MB; unusually large/detailed designs may need SVG or DXF. **Pattern export size** chooses XS–XL using the same grades as the tech pack and 3D size preview, independently of the saved sample and the size shown in 3D. An **outfit patterns and tech packs ZIP** downloads every applied outfit garment at its own sample size or, with **Outfit export size**, grades every piece independently to one chosen size from XS–XL, with SVG/DXF patterns, Excel and printable diagram-based tech packs, applied artwork and uploaded repeat images, and a manifest with source sample sizes, exported sizes, placement and repeat settings. It needs no drape, preserves the design and ignores temporary size previews or individual export grades; an unsupported grade refuses the whole bundle and names the garment; apply or discard drafts first. Individual downloads label the selected size; non-sample filenames include it. Sizes outside the supported measurements refuse with an explanation, and every skirt size uses its graded panels and band. Apply measurement drafts before exporting; the linked pattern previews stay at the sample size.
- **Printable tech pack**: its measurement tables, diagram and front/back images describe the applied sample size. While previewing a different size, the images use the sample styling shell.
- **Product flared skirt**: style its front/back with the same fabric, print and colour tools as tops. Apply waist, hem circumference and side-seam slant together; linked annular-sector patterns, a rigid 3D shell, tech pack, colorways/costing and PNG/GLB/SVG/DXF exports follow. Body & drape simulates it held at the waist using the shared product solver and fabric library. **Skirt waistband height** adds an independently styled folded band above the waist seam (2–10 cm; 0 removes it), keeping the panel slant unchanged. Its cutting strip is twice the finished height, with a fold line in SVG/DXF, and its outer surface is sewn into the drape and included in 3D/renders and the tech pack. Old unbanded designs stay unchanged. The band’s inner facing, fold contact, interfacing and closure are not simulated; choose a closure and any extension separately. XS–XL size columns use the same relative grades as the size view and pattern exports; the legacy dialog’s saved grading rule stays separate.
- **Measured two-panel skirt** (legacy dialog, preserved independently): waist/hem/slant input, optional waistband, seam and hem allowances, and linear size grading. Exports include a full-size SVG, A4/US Letter tiled print HTML, and rigid or draped OBJ. Its drape simulation uses the product solver and the fabric library; skirts saved with the earlier presets (chiffon, cotton, linen, denim) drape as their library equivalents. **Replace 3D product with this skirt** copies the applied waist, hem, slant and any finished waistband height to a product skirt with default linen styling in one undoable edit. Applied legacy body waist, hip and waist-to-hip depth copy with the skirt; other product body settings stay. With no applied legacy body, all product body settings stay unchanged. The main drape uses a stadium-shaped proxy instead of circular rings, so the hang can differ and a body waist too large for the skirt can refuse. Legacy closure extension, allowances, fabric and grading remain separately saved. The copy is not linked to later legacy edits.
- **Saved designs and versions** (dialog): keep up to 30 complete named copies of the design in this browser and reopen one as an undoable step. **Save version** saves a copy numbered for the design ("Oxford shirt · v3"), and **Compare with current** lists in plain words what has changed since a saved copy: the garment, measurements, neckline, hem and modules, fitted points of measure, each part's fabric, colour (by name) and print, artwork and colorway counts, shared body measurements, and the same garment settings for each of the outfit's other pieces. Automatic body dimensions are distinguished from measured ones. The comparison covers these settings rather than every item in the project backup.
- **Concept sketchbook** (dialog): tee sketching, a photo overlay, and PNG/SVG/JSON export. **Replace 3D product with this concept tee** scales its proportions from a chosen product chest measurement, previews the starting length and sleeves (bounded to the editable tee limits), and copies colour and neckline into an editable product in one Undo step. Soft stripes become an editable horizontal print; crosshatch illustration texture stays in the source. Optional body marks become one transparent front graphic, fitted within the body and at most 40 cm wide, editable in Artwork; sleeve marks stay in the sketchbook. Compatible outfit copies and shared body settings stay; incompatible removals ask first. The source concept remains independent. Freehand contours, photos and notes are not interpreted as construction or body measurements. Edits have Undo/Redo; consecutive title/note typing in one focused field is one step, preserving earlier garment edits. Leaving the field or making another edit starts a new step. Valid text still autosaves locally as you type, and a library holds up to 12 saved named copies. Autosave reuses checked images while you edit, avoiding repeated decoding of unchanged photos and graphics.

Trousers' **Ankle cuff height** makes them joggers: rib cuffs cut a third shorter than the leg opening gather it, the inseam is measured to the cuff edge (the leg panels stop the cuff's height short of it), and the cuffs are cut as two pattern pieces, listed in the tech pack with their points of measure and how they are set, and drawn in 3D, where draped legs gather into them at the ankle. "Joggers", "sweatpants" or "track pants" in a description do the same, and "shorts" takes the cuffs off.

Trousers and shorts can have **front pockets**, a **back yoke**, **back pockets** and a **fly front** with **belt loops**, as jeans do, each styled on its own. Each front pocket is a curved opening from the waist to the side seam (about a tenth of the hip across and a quarter of the rise down), cut out of the front pattern piece, with its facing behind it as a pattern piece of its own and in 3D, riding on the front leg through the drape; the tech pack measures the opening, sews it and lists pocketing for the bags. The yoke's seam crosses the seat below the waistband, about 3 cm deep at the side seams and 6 cm at the centre back, and the pointed patch pockets sit 1.5 cm below it, riding on the back legs through the drape. Both are topstitched in 3D with a double row (gold thread on denim, a shade darker than the fabric otherwise), cut as their own pattern pieces, and set in the tech pack with their points of measure. A fly closes the front legs with a zip under a fly topstitched in a J on the wearer's left, and fastens a straight waistband with a shank button, so the waist is fitted: each leg narrows from the hip line to the waist along its side and centre seams instead of being gathered into an elastic, and the drape puts it on a body 2 cm smaller at the waist. The fly's facings are cut on to the front legs; five belt loops, cut as one strip, sit round the waistband and ride on it through the drape. The tech pack lists the zip and button, measures the fly opening and the loops and sews them. The Jeans template has all of these, and "jeans", "front pockets" (or "scoop pockets"), "back pockets", "yoke", "zip fly" or "belt loops" in a description add them; "elasticated waist" takes the fly off.

Optional **underbust girth** gives the body a bust: the chest is then measured round the fullest part of the bust, the ribcage under it takes the underbust's girth, and two rounded forms in front of it are sized so that a tape round the bust measures the chest. The shoulders, arms and, without a height, the torso's lengths follow a frame between the two girths, and the automatic waist follows the ribcage. Tops and dresses drape over the bust and hang from it, in the mannequin, outfits, profiles, backups and fabric comparisons. Chest depth is left blank with a bust, which sets its own.

Optional **upper-arm girth** sets the upper arm on the proxy without narrowing it to fit the selected sleeve. Optional **neck girth** sets its circular neck without changing the garment neckline, head or shoulders. Both follow body profiles, project backups, outfits and matched fabric comparisons. Blank keeps automatic dimensions; forearms, wrists, shoulder slope and pose stay assumed, and this does not validate fit.

Optional **Shoulder to waist** sets the vertical distance from shoulder-point level to the natural waist, rather than deriving it from chest girth or height. The hip follows that waist with its existing or entered waist-to-hip distance. With a height entered, a longer torso shortens the estimated trouser legs to keep the shared outfit body's floor level fixed. It follows profiles, project backups, size previews and fabric comparisons, with Undo. Blank keeps previous proportions; shoulder placement, pose and physical fit remain approximate.

**Measure the body from photos** (Body & drape): a photo from the front, standing in close-fitting clothes with the arms held a little away from the sides, and your height give estimated chest, waist and hip girths, upper-arm girth, chest depth and waist-to-hip depth; a second photo from the side makes the girths more accurate. Google's MediaPipe pose model finds the person's outline on the device (it is downloaded when first used, about 6 MB, pinned and checked by SHA-256). Each girth is a straight-line fit, from the outline's width at its level and, with a side photo, its depth there, to tape measurements of 280 people in BodyM (Amazon). The photos show the outline found and where each was measured, a photo whose arms touch the body is refused with the reason, and the estimates go into the body fields as drafts to correct and apply. On the 68 measurable people of BodyM test A they were within 5 cm for 60% of chests, 74% of waists and 78% of hips with a side photo (47%, 44% and 72% from the front alone), and within 3 cm for 78% of upper arms. They were not tried on people photographing themselves at home.

Changing **Wear with** keeps an edited third garment when it is compatible with the new outfit. If it cannot stay, the studio asks before removing it. Changing the edited garment’s type by template or description also keeps compatible pieces and asks before removing any that no longer go with it.

Use **Edit worn garment** for a pair or **Garment to edit** for three pieces, including outfits built around the initial Blank tee. The applied copies and shared body settings stay together; edits affect only the current garment. Switching is one Undo step, requires applied/discarded product drafts, and returns 3D and pattern export sizing to the new garment’s sample. The saved-library source stays separate.

Fabric comparisons of a top worn with bottoms use the same resolved waist and hip girths as the outfit preview. The comparison isolates the top; it does not simulate contact with the other garment.

**Drape time** offers Standard (3 simulated seconds) and Longer (12 simulated seconds per garment). Changing it restarts the preview on the same applied design and body. It is a session setting, and pending drafts stay untouched. More settling can reduce motion, but it takes more computation and does not guarantee convergence or physical fit. Matched fabric comparisons have their own time choice and report the actual shared solve settings in the download.

## Known gaps

- Fabric properties (bending rigidity, membrane stiffness) are typical published values per fabric construction, not measurements of these fabrics. Bending is scaled per construction so each fabric's drape test lands mid-range in the coefficients reported for it (knits needed about three times their elastic rigidity, standing in for the yarn friction that holds real folds open); those ranges are approximate, and garments are not yet compared with reference drapes. Stretch depends on direction to the chosen per-part grain (wovens are soft on the bias, knits stretch more across), but the model is elastic: at garment-weight loads this barely changes the hang, and slow bias drop (creep) is not modelled. The 3D mesh is about 1.5-2 cm; a 1 cm mesh doubled the drape time without visibly changing the default tee, whose smooth hang comes from the T-posed proxy body rather than the mesh.
- Tops drape with the arms along the sleeves. Lowering the arms to a relaxed pose was tried: beyond about 50° below horizontal the underarm stretched by over 40%, because the solver pushes cloth to the nearest body surface and has no cloth-to-cloth contact, so it is not used.
- Trouser legs are straight and posed apart, and a tight front rise shows as stretch at the crotch point rather than as drag lines. The legacy skirt dialog shares the solver and fabric library but keeps its own body proxy (a surface of revolution); body and project-conversion unification remain unfinished. Cloth-to-cloth contact is limited to an outfit's top resting outside its bottoms, or tucked into them (a radial envelope round the body): tucked fabric is held in a thin layer rather than bunching, and in trousers the seat below the hip does not feel it, so a long top's tail can pass through the seat seam unseen; a hood lies over the back as a single layer, without contact with the body panels. The body is a proxy, not a personalized avatar: height, shoulder width, shoulder-to-waist and waist-to-hip distances and an underbust adjust its proportions, with remaining landmarks based on typical fractions (the bust points sit about 6 cm below the armpit, about 18 cm apart for a 100 cm bust, as two spheres rather than a measured shape); this is not a body scan.
- Every garment is meshed from its own pattern pieces, but those are simplified finished shapes, not graded production patterns: a top's front armhole scoops a fixed 1.2 cm deeper than the back near the underarm rather than being drafted from body measurements, the sleeve cap is a fixed symmetrical shape whose width is fitted to both armholes together, and hood and pocket shapes use fixed proportions. The hood's neck edge is drafted to the neckline's length; the hood starts folded back and drapes down the back. A polo's placket is drawn over the front in 3D with pictured buttons; the front pattern piece is not slit for it. Shirts have no sleeve plackets (a cuff is a closed band in 3D, its overlap only in the pattern), and a yoke covers the back only (no front yoke). Collar patterns share the flat draft used by the drape: woven collars have a wider fall, not a fully drafted tailored collar, and interfacing, turn-of-cloth and separate undercollar shaping remain unspecified.
- Descriptions are read by a keyword parser with a fixed vocabulary. Small local language models were tried and not adopted (see the direction doc), and logos, graphics other than quoted text, and construction details are not read.
- Dresses are long tops: a T-shirt dress can be shaped at the waist and cut there into a bodice and a skirt, which can be gathered into the seam but not pleated. Bust and waist darts sit at set places (a bust point set from the chest, the back darts a tenth of the chest from the centre) rather than ones drafted from body measurements, a waist dart opens on its side-seam side rather than evenly about its centre, darts through the waist end at set heights, and there is no woven dress block. Gathers form only as folds a few centimetres wide, as fine as the drape mesh allows. A dress hangs over a straight column below the hips (the legs together), so walking ease and slits are not modelled.
- Front and back artwork is projected straight on as seen from that side, so on bottoms its centre must lie on the legs or skirt as seen from there: it cannot wrap round a leg. Sleeve artwork wraps round the arm but cannot cross the sleeve's seams, and nothing goes on a hood, collar, cuff, waistband, jeans yoke or pocket. On the pattern they are marked as the 3D view prints them, so a traced print on bottoms is not a flat rectangle, and a print that crosses a seam is cut there.
- Jeans have no coin pocket or rivets, the fly's shield is the right front's cut-on facing rather than a separate piece, and the zip is not drawn (it is under the fly). The internal pocket bags are not simulated in 3D. Outfits are up to three garments (a top with bottoms, tucked in or not; a T-shirt under a sweatshirt, hoodie, button-up shirt or coat, or a sweatshirt under a coat; or both with bottoms), with no fully tailored jackets (a coat or blazer is a long shirt cut roomy, lined or not, without shoulder padding or chest canvas, and its lapels lie on the chest in a set roll rather than one the fabric and canvas hold; shawl lapels are not drafted; a hoodie does not go under it, as its hood would have to lie out over the coat's collar; the bomber is unlined and unpadded), and a garment underneath is not simulated against the one over it (it is draped first, then pressed in and kept inside where covered). Darts only on fitted skirts, and pleats only on skirts, where the fabric does not touch itself (the underlays are kept inside by layer order, not collision); gathers only at a skirt's waist, a dress's waist seam and a puff sleeve's cap, where the roughly 2 cm mesh shows them as soft flutes rather than fine gathers, no inference of freehand sewing geometry or a garment from a reference photo. Studio renders are drawn with WebGL in the browser, not path traced: shading in the folds is estimated on screen, and there is no translucency (a chiffon looks opaque), fibre detail beyond the fabric tiles, or photographed fabric scans.
- Reading a garment from a photo was tried on about 1,000 Fashionpedia photos (CC BY 4.0, used for evaluation only): rules on the pose model's landmarks and the colours along the limbs read the lower garment (trousers, shorts, skirt or dress) right 50% of the time and the sleeve length 68%, and CLIP ViT-B/32 zero-shot 73% and 78%. That is short of the 80% set for offering a starting point, so photos are not read into garments.
- Consumption, grading, construction steps and costing defaults are starting values. Confirm them with a marker, supplier quotes and a fit sample.
- An evaluation of the PPF Contact Solver did not lead to adopting it (see the direction doc). The app uses its own XPBD solver.

## Run locally

Prerequisites: Node.js 22.18+ (`engines`; CI uses 24) and npm. You need a current browser with WebGL, IndexedDB, Canvas and `createImageBitmap`. Without WebGL, pattern editing and garment GLB export still work; the interactive 3D view, PNG renders and turntable video require WebGL.

```sh
cd apps/clothing-studio
npm ci
npm run dev        # Vite dev server on 127.0.0.1; open the printed URL
```

Production build: `npm run build`, then `npm run preview`. The `dist/` output can be served by any static server. The production app makes no network requests.

npm run typecheck      # tsc --noEmit
npm run build          # typecheck + Vite production build

Use `CHROMIUM_PATH=/path/to/chromium` to run against an existing browser, and `CLOTHING_TEST_PORT` to change the port. CI: `.github/workflows/clothing-studio.yml` (`npm run check`, then the browser tests).

## Data & privacy

- Everything stays in the browser. The current project autosaves to IndexedDB (`clothing-studio`), and saved named copies go to a second IndexedDB database (`clothing-studio-designs`). Saved body profiles are kept in `localStorage`. Unapplied drafts and Undo history last only for the session.
- Uploaded photos are resized to at most 1200 px and re-encoded as JPEG without metadata. Motifs and artwork are size-checked before decoding. Nothing is uploaded. Photos for measuring the body are read in memory and not kept; their only network request is the pose model download.
- Saved designs, backups and body-profile exports contain your photos, artwork and measurements. Clearing browser data removes the local library, so keep downloaded JSON backups of work you want to keep. Several tabs share one autosave, and the last write wins.
